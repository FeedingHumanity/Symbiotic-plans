**The first-principles way is to treat the ISS as already-paid LEO feedstock, mechanically extract the highest-value large pieces, and use Starship’s existing high-capacity, refuelable architecture to move only those pieces (or minimally densified product) the remaining ~6 km/s to the lunar surface.**

### Core physics constraints
- Mass already in LEO has paid the ~9.3–9.5 km/s Earth-to-LEO cost. Every kilogram of Al, Ti, Cu, or Si recovered from the ISS therefore only needs the LEO-to-lunar-surface delta-v (~5.9–6.1 km/s including landing). This is the irreversible energetic advantage.
- The rocket equation is merciless: propellant mass grows exponentially with delta-v. Moving refined, high-density product is far cheaper than moving unrefined station mass or launching the same metals from Earth.
- Continuous free solar power exists in LEO; the Moon has long nights and lower insolation. Do as much sorting and any light processing as possible in LEO.
- Starship is the only vehicle with both the payload volume/mass and the demonstrated (or near-term) ability to soft-land large cargo on the Moon. Its architecture already requires orbital refueling; that same architecture can service a LEO-to-lunar cargo run.

### Minimal-energy sequence
1. **Propulsion independence + selective mechanical harvest**  
   Keep the station at operational altitude with Dragon-trunk or dedicated reboost kits. Use Optimus-class robots (or Canadarm2 + free-flyers) with cold mechanical cutters to remove the largest contiguous pieces of aluminum structure, titanium fittings, copper bus bars/cables, and intact solar-array sections. No melting, no furnaces, no powder. Secure the pieces on remaining structure or in a temporary storage module. This maximizes “largest pieces” while minimizing new debris and process energy.

2. **Staging without full Starship-ISS docking**  
   Full Starship is too large and its thruster plumes too energetic for direct docking to the current ISS. Practical alternatives that still obey first principles:
   - Soft-capture or berth a smaller intermediate vehicle (or a Starship nose section / cargo pod) to an existing IDSS port or a temporary truss attachment.
   - Transfer the harvested pieces robotically or by short free-flight hop.
   - Load them into a Starship cargo variant already in a nearby LEO parking orbit.

3. **Orbital refueling + lunar transfer**  
   One or more tanker Starships top off the cargo Starship in LEO (the same architecture already required for HLS). The cargo Starship then performs the trans-lunar injection, lunar-orbit insertion if needed, and powered descent to a pre-selected landing site near the South Pole or a future base. Because the payload is already dense metal, the propellant cost per useful kilogram is minimized.

4. **Lunar delivery and use**  
   Soft-land the large structural sections, bus bars, and solar-array pieces. They become immediate feedstock for habitats, radiators, power distribution, and shielding—skipping the early, energy-intensive lunar ISRU steps that would otherwise be required to extract the same metals from regolith.

### Why this is the first-principles minimum
- Energy is spent only on the remaining delta-v and on mechanical separation, not on re-lifting mass from Earth or on high-temperature processing in vacuum.
- Starship’s payload bay and landing capability are used at their natural scale; no new vehicle class is invented.
- The schedule advantage is large: early tons of refined metal accelerate closed-loop lunar industry far more than waiting for full ISRU maturity.
- Risk is bounded: progressive disassembly can stop at any point, residual mass can still be de-orbited, and no irreversible thermal processes are required.

Any more complex path (full melting in LEO, multiple intermediate depots, or trying to land the entire station) adds energy, mass, and failure modes without changing the fundamental gravity-tax arithmetic. The sequence above is the shortest logical chain from “mass already in LEO” to “useful metal on the lunar surface.”
